Research Philosophy

While economic theory sees prices as the market equilibrium between supply and demand, market microstructure theory sees price changes as a function of order flows.

$$R_t = f({O}_t) \quad \text{where} \quad R_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)$$

Even game theory, the study of decision-making between independent players, fits into this framework. In plain English, prices move because people trade, and people trade for different reasons.

We think of $f$ as an abstract function with a parameterization $\theta$, where the overall order flow ${O}_t$ is itself a function of a general market context $c_t$.

$$R_t = f_{\theta}({O}_t) \quad \text{where} \quad {O}_t = ({o}_{1t}, {o}_{2t}, \dots, {o}_{nt})$$

$$\text{and} \quad {o}_{it} = {h}_{i}({c}_t) \quad \text{for} \quad i = 1, \dots, n$$

To be more precise, ${O}_t$ is the aggregation of the order flows from all $n$ agents, each of whom reacts differently to a given context ${h}_{i}({c}_t)$.

If ${o}^{\text{obs}}_{it} \neq {h}_{i}({c}_t)$, we see it as a lack of information in ${c}_t$ rather than a lack of complexity in ${h}_{i}$. Our research is therefore a directed search over the conditioning space ${c_t}$.

Each candidate variable to be added to $c_t$ is measured by how much it reduces the uncertainty of a future flow $o_{it}$. This is where an understanding of $h_i$ comes in handy, as it can be used as a framework to reason about what is still missing in $c_t$.

By trying to predict $o_{it}$ individually, we implicitly think about $R_t$ as a superposition of order flows, transforming one hard problem into $n$ simpler problems.

This decomposition is not merely aesthetic. Individual flows are lower-dimensional, they are easier and more robust to forecast than $R_t$ itself. Forecasting $R_t$ implicitly requires forecasting the aggregate order flow $O_t$.

This is why starting from $R_t$ is not ideal, different combinations of aggregate flows can produce the same $R_t$ at a given instant, yet arise from different aggregate processes and therefore imply different future prices.

We think this level of abstraction is sensible because it applies across asset classes and time horizons, from options market making to global macro trading.

We aim to build systems that adapt under parameter uncertainty, circumventing the overfitting and wishful extrapolation that so often plague backward-looking and rule-based approaches.

What initially spans pages of first-principles reasoning is systematically distilled into essential lines of executable code and explanation. If we cannot render it with clarity, we have likely not truly understood it.
