Research Philosophy : Structure Over Statistics

While economic theory sees prices as the market equilibrium between supply and demand, market microstructure theory sees price changes as a function of order flows.

$$R_t = f({O}_t) \quad \text{where} \quad R_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)$$

Even game theory, the study of decision-making between independent players, fits into this framework. In plain English, prices move because people trade, and people trade for different reasons.

We think of $f$ as an abstract function with a parameterization $\theta$, where the overall order flow ${O}_t$ is itself a function of a general market context $c_t$.

$$R_t = f_{\theta}({O}_t) \quad \text{where} \quad {O}_t = ({o}_{1t}, {o}_{2t}, \dots, {o}_{nt})$$

$$\text{and} \quad {o}_{it} = {h}_{i}({c}_t) \quad \text{for} \quad i = 1, \dots, n$$

To be more precise, ${O}_t$ is the aggregation of the order flows from all $n$ agents, each of whom reacts differently to a given context ${h}_{i}({c}_t)$.

If ${o}^{\text{obs}}_{it} \neq {h}_{i}({c}_t)$, we see it as a lack of information in ${c}_t$ rather than a lack of complexity in ${h}_{i}$. Our research is therefore a directed search over the conditioning space ${c_t}$.

Each candidate variable to be added to $c_t$ is measured by how much it reduces the uncertainty of a future individual flow $o_{it}$. This is where an understanding of $h_i$ comes in handy, as it can be used as a framework to reason structurally about what is still missing in $c_t$.

By trying to predict $o_{it}$ individually, we implicitly think about $O_t$ as a superposition of individual order flows, transforming one hard problem into $n$ simpler problems. This decomposition is not merely aesthetic. Individual flows are lower dimensional, they are easier and more robust to forecast than $O_t$ itself.

Starting from $O_t$ is not ideal, different combinations of individual flows can produce the same $O_t$ at a given instant, yet arise from different individual processes and therefore imply different future ${O_{t+1}}$ and ${R_{t+1}}$.

This is the identification problem where multiple parameter sets produce the same distribution of observations and why we adopt a structural rather than a purely statistical approach. The difference between what a statistical model can recover from data and what actually generates the observations is the identification gap.

Consider two structural models. In Model $A$, Agent $1$ drives factor $1$ and Agent $2$ drives factor $2$. In Model $B$, Agent $1$ drives factor $2$ and Agent $2$ drives factor $1$. Both models produce the same covariance matrix. PCA cannot distinguish them, it returns the same eigenvectors for both. Yet the two models imply different futures. The eigenvectors are artifacts of the covariance matrix, not mechanisms. This is the identification gap.

This gap cannot be closed by more complex learning algorithms. It can only be closed by additional information from market mechanics and structural invariants, which are what we aim to add to $c_t$.

We think this level of abstraction is sensible because it applies across asset classes and time horizons, from options market making to global macro trading.

We aim to build systems that adapt under parameter uncertainty, circumventing the overfitting and wishful extrapolation that so often plague backward looking and rule based approaches.

What initially spans pages of first principles reasoning is systematically distilled into essential lines of executable code and explanation. If we cannot render it with clarity, we have likely not truly understood it.
